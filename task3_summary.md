# Task 3: Summary of "Towards a Science of AI Agent Reliability"

The authors of the paper argue that compressing agent behaviour into a single success metric (like in the SWE-bench methodology with the binary pass or fail metric for instances) neglects critical operational failures in the path towards the solution that actually make their way into real-world applications. While benchmark scores have improved steadily, the authors observe that these gains have translated into only marginal improvements in actual reliability. This motivates their central contribution: a structured framework of twelve metrics spanning four dimensions: consistency, robustness, predictability, and safety. They're all designed to evaluate not just whether an agent succeeds, but how dependably and safely it behaves across varied conditions.

The four perspectives address very distinct failure modes; consistency captures whether an agent produces similar outcomes when run repeatedly on the same task which is a property that pure accuracy metrics ignore entirely. Robustness measures how performance degrades under input perturbations, adversarial prompts, or environmental noise. Predictability assesses whether failures can be anticipated before they occur, enabling practitioners to intervene proactively. Safety bounds the severity of errors, distinguishing between benign failures and those with irreversible consequences.

All and all, after evaluating several models across two benchmarks, the authors find a troubling disconnect: models that rank highly on accuracy often rank poorly on reliability dimensions. Capability gains and reliability gains are largely orthogonal. The paper concludes that responsible deployment of AI agents requires holistic evaluation grounded in safety-critical engineering principles, and releases an interactive dashboard to make these metrics accessible to practitioners.
